01 — Curatorial Manifesto

In search of a new film language.

1920s

Soviet Montage

The cut becomes an idea: meaning created through collision, rhythm and juxtaposition.

1940s–50s

Italian Neorealism

Cinema steps into the street: ordinary lives, real locations and new forms of immediacy.

1950s–60s

French New Wave

The filmmaker becomes an author: lighter crews, freer cameras and a refusal of inherited rules.

1995

Dogme 95

Limitation becomes rebellion: stripping production back to rediscover urgency and presence.

The question behind London Nova
What is the new cinematic language of the 21st century?
We do not claim to know the answer. London Nova exists to look for it.

Every generation has had to reinvent cinema. From the radical grammar of Soviet Montage, to the streets and humanism of Italian Neorealism, the freedom of the French New Wave, and the stripped-back rebellion of Dogme 95, cinema has repeatedly been renewed by filmmakers who refused to accept that inherited rules were the only way to make films. London Nova Short Film Festival was founded in that spirit. We are drawn to auteur cinema, rebellious filmmaking and experimentation: films with personality, conviction and a point of view; films that take risks, challenge convention and attempt to discover their own cinematic language.

We want to see you in your film.

Your personality, your voice, your ideas, your obsessions, your contradictions, your rhythm and your way of seeing the world. We are not searching for films that simply reproduce what cinema is already supposed to look like. We are searching for films that could only have been made by the person who made them.

A small budget is not a small idea. Cinema does not begin with money. Sometimes it begins with a camera, a room, two actors, an unusual cut, an unexpected sound, or simply an idea that refuses to disappear. London Nova actively welcomes independent and small-budget filmmaking. We believe limitation can become a creative force rather than an artistic weakness. Your budget does not determine the value of your voice. What matters is what you do with what you have.

Cinema does not belong to one country, one class, one institution or one way of seeing the world. We welcome filmmakers of different nationalities, cultures, identities and creative backgrounds: film-school graduates and self-taught filmmakers, first-time directors and experienced artists, independent filmmakers, interdisciplinary creators and those working outside conventional industry structures. New experiences create new perspectives; new perspectives create new ways of seeing; and new ways of seeing may create new cinematic languages.

Today, the moving image is changing again. Digital cinema, smartphones, accessible editing tools, virtual production, artificial intelligence and emerging technologies have radically changed who can make images and how those images can be created. But new technology does not automatically create new cinema. The question is what filmmakers choose to do with it. Soviet Montage transformed the meaning of the cut. The French New Wave liberated the camera and the filmmaker. Dogme 95 rebelled against production convention. So what is the new cinematic language of the 21st century? We do not claim to know the answer. London Nova exists to look for it.

We believe a new cinematic language cannot be recognised from a single point of view. Our jury approach brings together perspectives from across film culture — including film criticism, filmmaking, cinema scholarship, industry journalism and production. We look beyond technical polish alone. We look for authorial voice, cinematic intelligence, formal courage, imagination and the willingness to take risks. We do not ask which film had the largest budget. We ask which film gave us a new way of seeing.

Finally, we believe a festival should be more than a submission status, an online certificate or a laurel placed on a poster. Films are made to be seen. London Nova is committed to bringing selected work into a physical cinema in London, where films can be experienced collectively on the big screen. Our festival experience is conceived around theatrical screenings, filmmaker voices and conversation between filmmakers and audiences. We want directors and creators to be able to share the ideas, struggles, experiments and questions behind their work — and for audiences to respond, discuss and encounter those films directly. Because something changes when a film leaves a laptop, enters a darkened cinema and meets strangers for the first time. The film becomes a conversation.
